Relationship regulation for neurodivergent couples

Out of sync isn't out of love.

Two nervous systems can run at different speeds and still fit together. You don't need to match tempos - you need to find where your rhythms meet.

That's not a metaphor about your relationship. It's the whole idea in one word.
What's actually happening

It's 6:30pm, and a text comes in. It's calm. Thoughtful. A good idea, even. And every alarm in one partner's body goes off anyway.

I think

"That's a thoughtful text."

I feel

"Like I'm missing part of the story."

My nervous system is clocking

"Calm after conflict used to mean a trap."

None of those three are lying. They're reporting from different lenses - logic, pattern-matching, old history. One partner's healthy way of calming down can look like withdrawal to the other. That reads as threat. That triggers a reaction. The reaction reads as threat right back.

Nobody did anything wrong here. Both people are trying. Neither feels safe. That's exactly what makes it so hard - and exactly what this site is for.

Three ideas carry everything here
  1. 1

    Different rhythms are not damage.

    Two nervous systems can run at different speeds and still fit together. Being out of sync is a timing problem, not a love problem.

  2. 2

    The asynchronous activation loop.

    When one partner regulates in a way the other reads as withdrawal, the second reacts - and the first reads that reaction as threat. The loop runs both ways. Naming it is the first step out. (An original framework, still in development.)

  3. 3

    Meet on the one.

    In a polyrhythm, independent patterns land together on a shared downbeat. We don't teach you to match tempos. We teach you to find - and protect - the moments where you meet.

This is written for you if
  • You or your partner are autistic, ADHD, or AuDHD, including mixed-neurotype couples.
  • Self-regulating alone comes easy to one of you and hard to the other, and that gap keeps getting misread.
  • One of you reaches for your partner to co-regulate under stress. The other reaches for quiet. One might land as rejection, the other might be "clingy or needy."
  • A trauma history shapes how one or both of you reads the other.
  • Sensory overload, masking, or shutdown changes how one or both of you show up at the end of a hard day.
  • You're a first responder, spouse or couple living on shift work and in a high-stress job.
About

Paramedic and writer. Helping neurodivergent couples find each other's rhythm.

Joshua Prull is a paramedic who also writes and makes music. His authority here comes from what he does, not from what happened to him - he spends his shifts keeping frightened people steady under pressure, one calm sentence at a time.

He's developing an original framework on asynchronous relational activation, aimed at peer-reviewed publication. The plain-language version of it is what this site teaches now. He also processes experience through the body rather than pictures, which is part of why these tools don't lean on visualization.

This is education, not therapy. Josh isn't a licensed clinician, and nothing on this site is individual counseling, treatment, or a diagnosis - for you or for your partner.
